Passage: When Instruments Become Interventions

A measuring instrument is usually imagined as a window: it may be cloudy, but polishing it should bring the
observer closer to a scene that exists independently of the glass. This metaphor works reasonably well when
a thermometer is placed briefly in a large reservoir. It becomes less reliable when measuring a phenomenon
alters the conditions that produce it. A sensor installed in a narrow stream may redirect the current; a survey
asking employees whether they trust management may itself change what employees regard as a
trustworthy response. In such cases, the problem is not simply that observations contain error. The object of
observation has partly become a product of the attempt to observe it.

Researchers sometimes address this difficulty by calibrating an instrument against a reference. But
calibration assumes that the reference remains stable under the relevant act of measurement. Consider a
municipal authority that estimates traffic congestion using travel times reported by a navigation application.
Initially, those reports may describe existing travel patterns fairly well. Once drivers receive route
recommendations based on the reports, however, traffic redistributes. A road formerly classified as
uncongested may become crowded precisely because the system classified it that way. Comparing the
application with a second application does not necessarily solve the problem if both influence drivers who
share the same roads.

It is tempting to call this a defect of technology and to prefer an observer who does not interfere. Yet
non-interference is not always available, nor is it always desirable. Public-health officials who measure
infection clusters may issue warnings that reduce transmission. If the resulting forecasts prove too
pessimistic, this need not show that the original model was poorly constructed: its publication may have
helped prevent the outcome it anticipated. The relevant distinction is between an instrument that
misdescribes a relatively stable world and one that helps change the world it describes. Both can produce a
mismatch between forecast and outcome, but the remedies differ. Better calibration may correct the former;
it cannot, by itself, explain the latter.

This distinction does not make every self-defeating forecast a success. A warning may be ignored, its
apparent effect may be due to an unrelated policy, or its initial forecast may have been exaggerated. To
claim that measurement caused a change, investigators need evidence about the pathway connecting
observation, response, and outcome. They might compare otherwise similar populations exposed to different
warnings, while recognizing that such comparisons can be compromised if people in one population
influence those in another. The task is therefore not to abandon standards of evidence but to make the
consequences of observation part of what those standards examine.

There is a further complication. An institution may deliberately choose a measurement that induces
desirable behavior rather than one that most accurately records behavior in the absence of measurement.
Publishing a hospital's waiting times might encourage hospitals to reduce delays, even if the published figure
then becomes a poor guide to how long a patient would have waited without publication. This can be a
defensible policy choice. It becomes intellectually misleading only when the institution presents the resulting
number as a neutral portrait of an untouched reality. The instrument can be both a useful lever and an

imperfect window; confusion begins when success in one role is treated as proof of success in the other.

Passage: The Accountability Gap in Automated Decisions

When an agency replaces a discretionary procedure with an algorithm, it often claims to have made
decisions more accountable. The claim has an intuitive basis. An official who rejects an application may offer
a reason that changes from one interview to the next; a computer system can apply the same rule to
thousands of applications and preserve a record of each calculation. Consistency and traceability are
genuine improvements. But neither establishes who should answer for a decision when its consequences are
challenged. A record of how a result was produced is not necessarily an account of why that result was
justified.

Consider a city that assigns emergency housing through a scoring system. Legislators specify that scarce
rooms should go to applicants facing the greatest risk. Administrators select the data used to represent risk,
a contractor develops the model, and caseworkers implement its rankings. If a person is excluded because
recent changes of address are interpreted as evidence of instability, each participant can plausibly point
elsewhere. The legislator authorized prioritization, not that particular indicator; the contractor optimized the
indicator supplied; the administrator relied on validation results; and the caseworker followed the prescribed
ranking. The decision is not ownerless because no one participated. It becomes difficult to contest precisely
because responsibility is distributed among participants whose contributions are individually defensible.

One response is to demand that algorithms be explainable. Yet an explanation may be technically accurate
while failing to identify the judgment at issue. A system might report that an applicant's score fell because
she had changed addresses three times. That tells us which feature affected the ranking, but not whether
frequent moves should count against a person whose displacement is itself evidence of need. Conversely, a
model that is too complicated to describe in a single sentence may still be governed by a publicly defensible
policy and a meaningful appeal process. Transparency is thus valuable, but it cannot substitute for deciding
which institution has authority to defend, revise, and remedy the rules embodied in a model.

Critics sometimes infer that responsibility requires returning every decision to a human official. This
overlooks two difficulties. Human discretion can conceal prejudice behind a vocabulary of judgment, and
placing an official at the end of an automated process may merely create a ceremonial signature. If that
official lacks the time, evidence, or authority to overturn the recommendation, the human presence does
little to restore accountability. What matters is not whether a person appears somewhere in the procedure,
but whether an identifiable actor can examine the grounds of a contested decision and change its outcome
when those grounds fail.

The strongest institutional arrangement may therefore separate routine consistency from exceptional
review. An automated system can rank ordinary cases while a designated body retains the authority and
resources to hear challenges, inspect the policy assumptions behind disputed indicators, and order
remedies. This arrangement is not costless: appeals take time, and exceptions can reduce uniformity. Nor
does it guarantee that every adverse result is mistaken. Its justification is narrower. Efficiency is worth
preserving where possible, but it should not be purchased by making a consequential decision impossible to
contest. Accountability is not the elimination of disagreement; it is the assignment of responsibility for
answering it.

Passage: The Uses and Limits of Public Memory

A city rebuilding its central square after a flood must decide whether to restore a statue that once stood
there. The statue commemorated an engineer credited with protecting the city from earlier floods; newly
examined records suggest that his embankments diverted water toward poorer settlements. Some residents
demand its removal as a correction to the historical record. Others insist that removal would erase the city's
past. Both positions presume that a monument chiefly communicates what happened. Yet a monument is
also a decision about which events deserve a permanent place in the present. Its significance lies as much in
the selection it performs as in the facts it depicts.

This distinction does not make accuracy irrelevant. A plaque that falsely attributes a bridge to the wrong
designer can and should be corrected. But even a perfectly accurate plaque cannot answer why one
designer is celebrated while the laborers who built the bridge remain unnamed. Historical inquiry asks
whether an account is supported by evidence; commemoration additionally asks what a community wishes
to honor. Treating these as identical tasks allows a political judgment to masquerade as a factual necessity.
It also permits officials to defend a controversial choice by pointing to details that no one disputes.

Museums sometimes respond by displaying contradictory accounts side by side. Such pluralism may expose
visitors to perspectives excluded from the official narrative. Yet adding voices does not automatically
redistribute authority. A museum may invite displaced families to recount the costs of an infrastructure
project while retaining sole control over which testimonies are shortened, translated, or placed beside the
entrance. The institution can become more inclusive in the material it exhibits without becoming more
accountable for how that material is arranged. Conversely, surrendering all editorial judgment would not
guarantee fairness: some claims are better supported than others, and curators must still distinguish
evidence from invention.

The problem is sharpened by institutions' incentives. A council may fund an exhibition that acknowledges
past injustice precisely because the acknowledgment demonstrates its present enlightenment. Public
criticism then becomes a resource for institutional legitimacy. This is not proof that the criticism is insincere
or ineffective. The exhibition may teach visitors something important, and officials may genuinely revise
their beliefs. But a measure of success confined to whether visitors admire the institution could reward the
appearance of self-criticism more readily than changes in whose interests the institution serves.

Consider a proposal to restore the statue but surround it with accounts of the settlements harmed by the
embankments. Opponents call the compromise evasive; supporters call it historically complete. Neither
verdict follows merely from the quantity of information supplied. The relevant questions include who chose
the accounts, whether residents can challenge their presentation, and whether the city treats the
documented harm as bearing on current flood policy. The statue might remain while the city's public
memory becomes more answerable to those it previously excluded; it might also remain as the centerpiece
of a display that absorbs criticism without altering anything consequential.

Public memory, then, should not be judged solely by the number of perspectives represented or the
accuracy of individual inscriptions. Its democratic quality also depends on the processes through which
representations can be disputed and on whether those disputes can affect decisions beyond the exhibition
hall. This does not mean every historical grievance must dictate a present policy. It means that institutions
cannot claim to have settled a controversy simply by displaying it.

Passage: The Ecology of Reliable Cooperation

A fishing cooperative and a private fleet can agree that the health of a coastal fishery matters and still
disagree about what to do this season. The private fleet calculates that a larger catch will cover its rising fuel
costs. The cooperative fears that an unusually large catch will reduce next year's breeding stock. Neither
side must be ignorant or malicious. The disagreement arises partly because the benefit of restraint is
distributed across future users, while the cost of leaving fish uncaught falls immediately on whoever
restrains themselves. Yet describing the problem as a simple conflict between individual profit and collective
welfare obscures a complication: a rule that makes restraint attractive in an ordinary year may cease to do
so after a storm destroys half the boats.

Governments often respond with fixed catch limits, treating the ecosystem as a stock whose renewal can be
estimated and divided into permissible withdrawals. Such limits can prevent a race to harvest. Their
apparent precision, however, rests on estimates of breeding rates, migration and illegal catches. If warmer
currents move spawning grounds, a limit derived from last decade's data may be too generous even when
every vessel complies. Conversely, a limit set conservatively enough for the worst imaginable season may
push small operators out of business in ordinary years. Scientific uncertainty therefore does not make limits
useless; it makes the conditions under which they are revised as important as the numbers initially chosen.

One proposed improvement is to allow unused catch allowances to be carried forward. In principle, this
rewards fleets that fish less today. But a transferable allowance can also become an asset: firms with access

to credit may purchase allowances from struggling operators, concentrating the right to fish even if
total extraction remains unchanged. A rule can thus improve the ecological indicator it was designed to
control while undermining the social cooperation on which enforcement depends. This is not an argument
that trading allowances is always harmful. Where ownership is dispersed and enforcement trusted, trading
may reduce the cost of meeting a conservation target. Its consequences depend on institutions that are
not visible in a chart of total catches.

A second proposal replaces annual limits with a trigger: fishing pauses whenever a monitoring index falls
below a threshold. Because the rule responds to current conditions, it appears more adaptive. But the index
itself may be affected by the rule. When fishing pauses, vessels stop supplying observations from offshore
waters; the resulting scarcity of reports can make the index look worse, prolonging the pause. Alternatively,
fleets may change where they fish to avoid triggering the threshold, leaving the index stable while pressure
shifts to poorly monitored habitats. A feedback mechanism is not automatically self-correcting merely
because it updates frequently. It can amplify an error or move the activity it measures out of view.

Some analysts conclude that conservation rules should be negotiated locally, since fishers possess
knowledge that remote models overlook. Local knowledge is indispensable, but locality does not abolish
power. A village committee dominated by boat owners may exclude seasonal workers whose livelihoods
depend on processing the catch. Equally, a distant regulator may protect those workers' interests while
misunderstanding the tides. The choice is not between local wisdom and scientific expertise; it is between
arrangements that can expose their assumptions to challenge and those that cannot. The relevant question
is who can contest a forecast, what evidence forces a revision, and who bears the cost while officials decide.

Reliable cooperation, then, is not simply the achievement of a low catch in a given year. It is the capacity to
maintain ecological restraint through shocks without treating every revision as a betrayal, and without
distributing the burden so unevenly that compliance collapses. A policy may succeed by its original metric
and still erode that capacity. Conversely, revising a target after new evidence need not indicate failure,
provided the process preserves reasons for those asked to comply to trust the rule. The durability of
conservation depends not on eliminating uncertainty, but on governing the consequences of being wrong.

Two-response directed network

Six stations A, B, C, D, E and F are connected by one-way roads. Two emergency teams depart
simultaneously at time zero: Team 1 starts at A and must reach E; Team 2 starts at B and must reach F.
Each team must follow a simple directed path (no station may be visited twice by the same team).

The table lists every available road and its traversal time in minutes. Reverse travel is prohibited unless
separately listed.

Road                   Minutes               Road                   Minutes                Road                  Minutes

A→B                    3                     A→C                    4                      A→D                   7

B→C                    2                     B→D                    3                      B→E                   8

C→B                    1                     C→D                    2                      C→E                   6

C→F                    9                     D→E                    3                      D→F                   5

E→C                    2                     E→F                    3                      F→E                   2

Capacity rule: A road may be assigned to at most one team for the entire operation, even if the teams would
traverse it at different times. The teams may share stations, including transit stations, but may not share a
directed road. All times are additive. Waiting never improves a solution and is excluded.

Dispatch objective: First minimize the time at which both teams have reached their destinations (the larger
of the two arrival times). Among assignments attaining this minimum, minimize the sum of the two arrival
times. If there is still a tie, no further tie-break applies. A question about the optimal dispatch refers to this
two-level objective.

Each question is independent and uses the original road network unless it explicitly describes a closure. A
closure removes the indicated road(s) entirely. Both teams must still be dispatched.

Conditional scheduling and rotating supervisors

A training centre must conduct six distinct workshops, labelled A, B, C, D, E and F, in six consecutive, equally
long time slots numbered 1 through 6. Exactly one workshop occupies each slot, and every workshop is
conducted exactly once.

Three supervisors, X, Y and Z, are available in a fixed repeating rotation: X supervises slots 1 and 4, Y
supervises slots 2 and 5, and Z supervises slots 3 and 6. A workshop's supervisor is determined by its
scheduled slot. Supervisors do not change slots when the workshop order changes.

The following six scheduling conditions all hold:

1. A occurs earlier than C.

2. B is scheduled exactly two slots after D (there is one intervening slot).

3. E is immediately adjacent to A, either before or after it.

4. If C occurs after B, then F must occur before A. This is a one-way implication; its converse is not assumed.

5. The slot numbers of A and F differ by at least three.

6. C and D are not in adjacent slots.

changes one. A modified-condition question does not inherit modifications from another question.
